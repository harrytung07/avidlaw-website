**A Comprehensive Guide to Commercial Real Estate Transactions in
British Columbia**

Commercial real estate transactions are significantly more complex than
their residential counterparts. They often involve multiple layers of
legal, financial, operational, and regulatory
considerations—particularly when business assets, leases, service
agreements, environmental liabilities, or corporate structures are part
of the deal. At Avid Law Corporation, we assist buyers, sellers,
developers, lenders, and investors in navigating these sophisticated
transactions with clarity and confidence.

This guide outlines the major legal issues that commonly arise in
commercial real estate transactions in British Columbia, drawing on
leading statutes, case law, and best practices for practitioners and
business owners alike.

**1. The Commercial Purchase Contract: Foundational Considerations**

The purchase contract is the backbone of any commercial real estate
transaction. Unlike residential transactions where standard-form
agreements are typically used with relatively minor adjustments,
commercial agreements must be tailored to the specific property,
business, and parties involved.

**Identifying the Proper Parties**

One of the first questions in any commercial deal is: Who is the true
vendor and purchaser?  
Commercial properties are often held through corporations, partnerships,
nominee companies, and bare trusts. The purchase contract should require
the vendor to represent and warrant whether it is both the legal and
beneficial owner. Where title is held by a nominee, the beneficial
owner—not the nominee—is the true vendor.

**Bare Trusts and Nominee Title Holders**

If the registered owner is a bare trustee, the agreement must clarify
whether:

- legal title will be transferred,

- beneficial interest will be transferred,

- or the shares of the nominee company will be sold.

Although share transfers may avoid property transfer tax, they carry
risks, including inheriting liabilities associated with the corporation
and triggering disclosure obligations under the *Land Owner Transparency
Act* (LOTA).

**2. Assignments of Contract**

While agreements can be freely assigned at common law, most commercial
contracts expressly restrict assignment. Vendors will often require:

- written consent,

- covenant that the original purchaser remains liable, and

- an assumption agreement from the assignee.

Updates in the Real Estate Services Regulation require standard
contracts prepared by licensees to state that contracts cannot be
assigned without written consent and that any assignment profit belongs
to the original seller—though this does not apply to pre-sale
assignments, which are governed by the *Real Estate Development
Marketing Act* (REDMA).

**3. Foreign Purchasers and Regulatory Compliance**

Transactions involving foreign entities require additional scrutiny.
*The* *Investment Canada Act* may require review or approval for certain
acquisitions. The *Business Corporations Act* (BCA) contains provisions
dealing with extraprovincial registration, and the Land Title Office
imposes documentary requirements for foreign corporations acquiring
land.

Foreign buyers acquiring residential property may also be subject to:

- the 20% Additional Property Transfer Tax (Foreign Buyers’ Tax);

- the federal Prohibition on the *Purchase of Residential Property by
  Non-Canadians Act* (Foreign Buyer Ban), extended to 2027—but clarified
  not to apply to commercial properties;

- limited partnership exemptions introduced in 2020.

Because commercial real estate can include residential-classified
properties (e.g., apartment buildings), careful tax and zoning analysis
is essential.

**4. Identifying the Property and Related Assets**

Commercial real estate transactions rarely involve land alone. The sale
may include:

- leases and lease guarantees,

- service contracts and equipment leases,

- intellectual property such as names, trademarks, phone numbers, or
  domain names,

- furniture, fixtures, equipment, and other chattels,

- business goodwill,

- municipal permits and approvals,

- architectural and engineering drawings,

- warranties and construction contracts.

Each asset must be reviewed individually. Proper assignment documents,
consents from third parties, and representations regarding the status of
assets should be negotiated and included in the purchase contract or
closing documents.

**5. Leases in Commercial Transactions**

Leases are often among the most important components of a commercial
real estate purchase. The purchaser assumes the role of landlord and
requires comprehensive disclosure about tenant relationships.

**Key Due Diligence Areas**

- tenant default history or disputes;

- rent deferrals, abatements, or free-rent periods;

- status of tenant inducements;

- completeness of lease files;

- previous assignments or subleases;

- commissions paid or outstanding.

Many purchase contracts require tenant estoppel certificates, which
lenders also frequently demand. These certificates confirm the tenant’s
understanding of the lease, status of payments, and absence of disputes.

**Unregistered Leases and Subdivision Issues**

The *Land Title Act* provides that leases under three years with actual
occupation bind purchasers without registration, while leases of
unsubdivided land present their own complexities following cases such as
*International Paper Industries Ltd. v. Top Line Industries Inc.,* 1993
CanLII 821 (BCCA), *Idle-O Apartments Inc. v. Charlyn Investments Ltd.*,
1980 CanLII 563 (BCSC) and *Marine Masters International Ltd. v. Canada
(Attorney General),* 2008 BCCA 541. A purchaser may need to require
direct agreements with tenants to ensure privity of contract.

**Impact of Competition Act Amendments (2024)**

New amendments to the *Competition Act* significantly restrict exclusive
use clauses and restrictive covenants in commercial leases. Clauses that
limit competition—such as prohibiting competing businesses in a shopping
centre—may be invalidated or penalized by the Competition Bureau. This
affects both new and existing leases, and purchasers must review all
restrictive covenants for compliance.

**6. Service Contracts, Equipment Leases, and Project Documents**

Purchasers must review all operational agreements associated with the
property. These may include:

- elevator maintenance contracts,

- janitorial or waste removal agreements,

- HVAC and equipment leases,

- property management agreements.

Many include automatic renewal or require long notice periods for
termination. The purchase contract should specify which agreements the
purchaser will assume and which the vendor must terminate.

**Project Documents**

In redevelopment scenarios, project documentation is critical.
Purchasers should require delivery or assignment—where permitted—of:

- architectural and engineering drawings,

- construction and warranty documents,

- occupancy and building permits,

- development and variance permits.

Failure to obtain these documents can result in significant unexpected
costs.

**7. Employment and Labour Issues**

Purchasing commercial real estate often means acquiring part or all of a
business. Employment consequences vary depending on whether employees
are unionized or non-unionized.

**Non-Union Employees**

At common law, employment contracts cannot be assigned. Unless
terminated, employees are deemed dismissed and may be owed severance.
However, under s. 97 of the *Employment Standards Act*, if the purchaser
hires the employees, employment is deemed continuous—making the
purchaser responsible for statutory vacation and certain future
obligations.

**Unionized Employees**

Under s. 35 of the *Labour Relations Code*, the purchaser is deemed a
successor employer and is bound by the collective agreement. Employees
cannot be automatically transferred; they may elect to stay with the
vendor (a “Verrin election”). Vendors must also provide 60 days’ notice
to unions under s. 54.

These issues must be negotiated carefully in the purchase contract.

**8. Purchase Price Allocation and Adjustments**

Commercial purchase price allocation affects taxes, GST treatment,
depreciation, and future capital gains. Parties should agree on
allocations for:

- land,

- buildings,

- equipment and chattels,

- goodwill.

Other adjustments include:

- rent and security deposits,

- prepaid expenses,

- rent arrears,

- tenant improvement allowances,

- operating cost adjustments,

- leasing commissions,

- taxes, utilities, and service contracts.

Disputes around rent arrears are common; contracts must allocate credit
and collection responsibility clearly.

**9. Environmental Due Diligence and Liability**

Environmental issues are often among the most significant risks in a
commercial transaction.

**Representations and Warranties**

Purchasers typically seek assurances regarding:

- compliance with environmental laws;

- absence of contamination;

- clean history of spills, discharges, or hazardous materials;

- valid permits and approvals.

Vendors resist broad warranties and often cap liability or limit
survival periods.

**As-Is / Where-Is Transactions**

In some cases, properties are sold strictly “as is-where is”, meaning:

- no environmental representations or warranties;

- purchaser relies solely on its own investigations;

- purchaser accepts future environmental liability;

- vendors may seek releases or indemnities.

**Site Disclosure Statements**

Under the *Environmental Management Act* (EMA) and the *Contaminated
Sites Regulation*, vendors must provide a site disclosure statement when
the property has had specified industrial or commercial uses. Certain
exemptions exist (e.g., primarily residential properties). Submission of
a site disclosure statement can automatically trigger site
investigations and require government approval before development or
subdivision.

**10. Searches, Investigations, and Title Issues**

Comprehensive due diligence is essential. Purchaser counsel should
undertake:

- zoning and by-law compliance checks;

- review of tenant files;

- review of service contracts and equipment leases;

- survey certificates;

- statutory lien searches;

- court registry litigation searches;

- bankruptcy searches;

- CRA tax searches (including filings for nominee entities);

- Personal Property Registry searches;

- environmental searches and audits;

- Land Owner Transparency Registry searches;

- corporate registry searches for good standing;

- confirmation of water licences, Crown leases, and other appurtenant
  rights.

**11. Special Considerations: Nominee Purchases and LOTA**

Purchasers acquiring shares of a nominee company instead of registering
legal title must understand the limitations of being a beneficial,
rather than registered, owner. Cases such as *BC Retail Partners*
(Boitanio Mall) confirm that beneficial owners may not enjoy the same
protection as registered owners.

LOTA imposes additional disclosure obligations whenever beneficial
ownership changes—even if legal title does not. Failure to comply may
result in significant administrative penalties or offences.

**Conclusion**

Commercial real estate transactions involve a wide spectrum of legal
considerations—from corporate structuring and trust arrangements to
leases, environmental liability, employment matters, tax consequences,
and regulatory compliance. A well-structured purchase contract and
thorough due diligence are critical to protecting the interests of all
parties involved.
